# Real-Business Copywriting (Layer 3)

How to write copy for an actual business so the site reads like it was written *for
this client*, not assembled from marketing filler. This is the reference for **stage
03 (Content)**. It sits next to the honesty rule in `anti-slop-rules.md §6` — design
has its anti-slop contract; this is the equivalent for words.

The one-line test: **could this paragraph appear, unchanged, on a competitor's
site?** If yes, it's filler — rewrite it with this client's specifics.

---

## 1. Principles

- **Clarity beats cleverness.** A visitor should understand what the business does,
  for whom, and what to do next within seconds. Never make them decode a slogan.
- **Specific, not generic.** Concrete nouns and real details ("Saturday clinics in
  Fareham", "20-minute fitting") over abstractions ("quality service", "customer
  focus"). Specificity is what makes copy sound human and trustworthy.
- **Customer's words, not the company's.** Speak to the problem they're trying to
  solve and the outcome they want, in the language they'd use. Drop insider jargon.
- **One voice throughout.** Match the tone adjectives from the brief and hold them
  across every page — homepage, services, contact, meta descriptions.
- **Active and direct.** "Book a fitting" not "Fittings can be booked." Short
  sentences. Cut hedging and throat-clearing ("We are pleased to…", "Welcome to…").

---

## 2. The value proposition (write this first)

Before any page copy, draft the **UVP**: a clear, concrete promise that connects the
customer's biggest problem to this business's specific solution. It anchors the hero
and the whole site's messaging.

A workable shape: **[who it's for] + [the outcome] + [why this business] (+ the
specific proof or differentiator).** Keep it a plain promise, not a tagline. If the
brief doesn't give you a real differentiator, ask for one — don't invent a superlative.

Avoid empty UVP words: *world-class, leading, premium, cutting-edge, passionate,
seamless, solutions, synergy, next-level, game-changing.* They signal nothing.

---

## 3. Structure frameworks (pick per section, don't template the page)

Frameworks shape a *section*; they are not a page layout. Vary which you use so the
page doesn't march in one rhythm (that mirrors the structural-variety rule on the
design side).

- **PAS — Problem · Agitate · Solution.** Name the reader's problem, show why it
  matters/costs them, then present the offer as the resolution. Strong for services
  that fix a pain (pain relief, repairs, urgent help).
- **AIDA — Attention · Interest · Desire · Action.** Hook, build relevance, make it
  desirable, then the CTA. Good for a hero or a flagship offer.
- **FAB — Feature · Advantage · Benefit.** Translate each feature into the concrete
  benefit *to this customer*. Stops a features list from reading like a spec sheet.
- **BAB — Before · After · Bridge.** Where they are now → where they'll be → how the
  business gets them there. Good for transformations (treatment plans, redesigns).

For a page, choose the *simplest* shape that fits the brief and let sections differ.

---

## 4. Section-by-section guidance

- **Hero** — UVP as the headline (a clear promise, not "Welcome"), a one-line
  subhead that adds the specific *who/what/where*, and one primary CTA. ≤ ~60ch
  headline, ~2 lines of lede.
- **Services / offer** — lead each with the customer benefit, then the detail. Real
  names, real scope. No invented prices unless the brief gives them (`[NEEDS: price]`
  otherwise).
- **About** — the real story and credentials from the brief. If the business is one
  person, say so — don't manufacture a "team" or "reception".
- **Social proof** — use **only** real, supplied testimonials/reviews/logos. If none
  are provided, omit the section or mark `[NEEDS: testimonials]`. Never fabricate a
  quote, a name, a star rating, or a client logo.
- **CTA** — one consistent action and voice across the site ("Book a fitting",
  "Request a quote"). Make the next step and what happens after it obvious.
- **Contact** — real, supplied details only: address, phone, email, hours. Mark any
  unknown as `[NEEDS: …]`.
- **Microcopy** — labels, button text, form helper text, empty states, error
  messages. These carry the voice too; write them, don't leave defaults.

---

## 5. Honesty (hard rule — mirrors anti-slop §6)

Use **only** facts the brief supplies. **Never invent** metrics, testimonials,
client names, prices, credentials, qualifications, years-in-business, awards, team
members, addresses, or "trusted by" logos. Where a real fact is needed but unknown,
write a clearly labelled `[NEEDS: …]` placeholder and list it under "Open questions"
for the human — do not fabricate, and do not hedge a guess as if it were fact.

No lorem ipsum, ever. Every word is shippable or an explicit labelled placeholder.

---

## 6. SEO meta & accessibility-adjacent copy

- **Title tag** per page: primary keyword + business + place where natural, ~50–60
  chars, unique per page.
- **Meta description**: a real, specific summary with the benefit and a reason to
  click, ~150–160 chars. Not keyword stuffing.
- **Headings** carry meaning and structure, not just styling — they're the page's
  outline for both readers and screen readers.
- **Alt-text intent**: note what each meaningful image should convey so stage 05
  writes real `alt`, not filenames.
- **Link text** is descriptive on its own ("See our fitting service", not "click
  here") — better for users, screen readers, and SEO.

---

## 7. Output

Organise `content.md` by page, then by section, in the **exact sitemap order**, so
stage 05 can drop copy straight into the build. Lead the file with the agreed UVP so
design (stage 04) and build (stage 05) share one north-star message. End with the
"Open questions" list of every `[NEEDS: …]`.
